02 / A reason to participate

Keep what works.
Make the difficult parts easier.

We should not ask operators to change their routine just because a newer system exists. The change needs to solve something that matters to them.

The direction we want to protect

Your business remains yours.

  • Your business name and existing customer relationships.
  • A say in which work you accept and the price you agree.
  • Useful relationships with other drivers, associations and referral partners.
  • The option to build around methods that already work, rather than replace them all at once.
Improvements worth investigating

Less uncertainty. Better opportunities.

  • Requests with the details needed to judge the job.
  • Fewer calls just to establish who is available or where a pickup stands.
  • A clearer way to arrange return rides, groups and work shared with other operators.
  • A useful presence in front of guests who are looking for the service you actually provide.

These are design goals, not verified findings or promised results. We need operators to tell us which problems they actually have, and which of these changes would help.

Participation has to mean something practical

Agree what work you take. Keep availability accurate enough to rely on. Accept or decline requests. Update important changes. We need to find the simplest way to do that during a real working day.

Who can join, how requests are shared, protection of existing referrals, and any exclusive access or territory remain questions to agree. No exclusivity is promised here.

03 / A reason for guests to use it

Being online is a start.
Being useful is the reason to return.

We need both sides: operators who can deliver the service, and guests who have a reason to find and choose it.

FIND / CHOOSE / RETURN

Which guest?
Which need?
Why this service?

One clearly defined problem is a better starting point than trying to offer everything to every visitor.

  1. Find it

    Give the right people a way to discover it.

    Search and business listings, accommodation referrals, existing contacts, or carefully tested advertising are possible routes. A Google Business Profile is one option to investigate where eligible, not a guarantee of enquiries.

  2. Choose it

    Make the benefit easy to understand.

    A local ride, an arrival pickup, or a return already arranged. Start with a need operators can reliably meet, and explain what is clearer or easier than the alternatives.

  3. Use it again

    Deliver what the guest was told to expect.

    Accurate information and a dependable ride give the guest a reason to come back or recommend it. The website alone cannot create that experience.

These are routes to test, not a completed marketing plan. Advertising costs, demand, competing services and the effort needed to win a booking still need checking.

04 / Keep the money clear

The trip income.
The extra work.
The platform.

Those are different things. Your existing driving business, a possible coordination role, and the software business should not be treated as one pot of money.

01

Your driving business

You earn from trips you carry out. More completed work, better use of time, or lower costs could help. A new fee on the same work, with no added benefit, would not.

02

Any additional role you take

Introductions, operator coordination or ongoing support could be separate work. Whether you want that role, what it involves and how it is paid would need agreement first.

03

The platform business

The product needs a sustainable way to fund its work and ongoing operation. A booking fee is one model to explore, not a price or arrangement that has been decided.

05 / What each of us brings

You know the work.
I can help make the idea testable.

You do not need to become the technical person. Your judgement about the work, and your ability to explain what operators need, are central to whether this is useful.

Your side of the exploration

Experience, questions
and connections.

Help us make sense of the working day. Challenge what does not fit. Tell me what you mean, what I have missed, and which questions other operators should answer for themselves.

If you are comfortable making introductions, help explain the idea accurately and invite voluntary feedback. You are not being asked to promise a finished service, guarantee bookings, or sell it to your colleagues.

My side of the exploration

Research, structure
and a working example.

Turn what we learn into clear options. Check what can be verified publicly, design the guest and operator experience, build examples, and explain the practical limits and costs.

Bring the choices back to you in a form we can discuss. Not decide the business in your name, or assume a technical solution is automatically the right answer.

Agree the work before taking it on

No upfront cash contribution is proposed from you for this initial exploration. That is not an agreement to unlimited unpaid work from either of us. Time, expenses, responsibilities, compensation, ownership and any continuing role need their own clear agreement.

A simple way to explain the idea to another operator
We're exploring a way to make arranging rides clearer for guests and the operators doing the work. The example shows what might be possible; it is not a service already taking bookings. We'd like to understand how your business works, what you already use, and what would genuinely make the work easier. Your experience will help us judge what is useful and what we should change.

Use this as a starting point for the conversation, not a script that replaces listening.

06 / Learn enough to choose a small test

Start with understanding.
Not a sales pitch.

We need to learn what is actually happening before deciding what the service should change. Feedback is useful even when it tells us our idea is wrong.

Check what we can ourselves

Do the background work.

Publicly described services, published fees and rules, guest-facing booking options, and requirements from the relevant authorities can help us ask better questions.

That research has not been completed for this walkthrough. A public rule is not proof of how a particular operator works or what they actually pay.

Ask what only operators can tell us

Listen to the working reality.

Where their work comes from. Which services they use and currently pay for. How often costs arise. How requests are accepted, shared or lost. What keeping availability current would involve. What they wish were easier.

This is not a test of whether they would buy our service. We do not need private customer lists, guest names, account details or copies of personal documents.

  1. Listen

    Walk through a recent booking and hear from willing operators with different ways of working.

  2. Choose

    Pick one recurring problem, a specific guest need, and a small group able to try a different approach.

  3. Agree

    Set the roles, limits, costs, support and permissions before any live trial. Check the requirements that apply.

  4. Test & review

    Look at completed trips, failures, operator effort, guest experience and the actual money left after costs. Then decide together whether to continue.
